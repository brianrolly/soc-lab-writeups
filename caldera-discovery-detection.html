What Three Emulations Caught — and What They Missed

Three CALDERA operations against a monitored Linux host, escalating from first-minutes discovery to a complete collection-and-exfiltration chain. auditd captured every command. Wazuh alerted on two stages out of seven — and the gap list turned out to be the valuable half.

Emulation CALDERA · Sandcat Telemetry Linux auditd Operations 3 9 rules · 8 techniques

Overview

Perimeter and SIEM detections watch traffic; they do not watch what an intruder types once they are on a host. This is the endpoint layer — three adversary-emulation runs against a Linux host under Wazuh and auditd, each one harder than the last. The first proved the detections fire. The second proved some of them fire at the wrong things. The third ran a full exfiltration chain and showed exactly where the coverage ran out.

The campaign

Three operations, escalating

Each run used a CALDERA Sandcat agent running as root on the victim host, beaconing to a C2 server on the lab's attack box.

OP-001 · the emulation

Discovery, and a binary pretending to be Splunk

A Sandcat agent executed a realistic reconnaissance sequence as root. The agent binary itself was planted at /root/splunkd — masquerading as a legitimate logging daemon, which is the detail worth catching (T1036).

victim-linux1 · auditdop-001 · 37 steps · agent running as root, elevated
whoami                     # who am I
cat /etc/passwd            # local accounts
ps aux | grep ...          # running processes  (34 executions)
groups                     # group membership
hostname ; uname -a        # system identity
ip addr ; ip route         # network config
the tellA process named splunkd that lives in /root rather than a package path, spawning discovery commands. The name is meant to survive a glance at ps; the path is what gives it away.

OP-001 · why 34 of 37 steps were the same command

The agent chained its own discoveries

The step count looks like noise until you read the order. Step 2 harvested every local username; steps 3–36 then hunted each one's processes in turn. CALDERA fed the output of one ability into the parameters of the next — the same thing a human operator does, only patiently and in alphabetical order.

OP-002 · the uncomfortable result

Five rules were watching the SIEM, not the attacker

The second operation ran CALDERA's Collection adversary through the atomic planner: search the filesystem for small media and config files, stage them, clean up. Wazuh detected the search correctly. It also produced a pile of alerts that had nothing to do with the emulation.

The structural lesson

Correlation rules inherit the noise of their children. Clusters 100301–100306 looked like strong signal until the child rules underneath them were traced back to cron and snapd. Tune children first, then re-evaluate the cluster — never the other way round.

The scoping fix

Filtering on data.audit.cwd=/opt/caldera-agent separated emulated activity from environmental activity cleanly. A detection that cannot distinguish the attacker from the monitoring agent is not yet a detection.

Ground truth beat the dashboard

archives.json was consistently more reliable than dashboard visualisations for establishing what actually executed. The dashboard shows what alerted; the archive shows what happened.

OP-003 · Super Spy

A complete exfiltration chain, scored stage by stage

The third operation ran CALDERA's Super Spy adversary end to end: create a staging directory, hunt for files, copy them in, compress the result, upload it to C2, then erase the evidence. Seven stages. Every one of them landed in auditd. Two of them alerted.

The mislabel worth fixing

Rule 100214 fired on the exfiltration — but its description reads “possible ingress tool transfer”. This was outbound: curl -F data=@… to /file/upload. An analyst triaging that alert would have been told the opposite of what happened. A rule that fires on the right event and describes the wrong direction is a false sense of coverage.

The shape of the gap

Every miss sits between collection and exfiltration — exactly the window where an intrusion becomes a breach. Discovery is loud and well covered; the quiet middle was not covered at all.

Why the misses are the deliverable

All five missed behaviours were present in archives.json. Telemetry was never the problem — rule coverage was. That distinction decides whether the fix is a new sensor or a new rule, and here it was unambiguously a rule.

What changed

The rules the gaps produced

All five are deployed and validated — not proposals.

T1074.001

Local staging detection — alert on mkdir of a staging directory and cp into it from an agent working directory, the pattern common to every collection framework.

T1560.001

Archive-before-exfil — tar/gzip producing an archive inside a directory that just received copied files. The sequence is the signal, not the utility.

100214

Split ingress from egress — a higher-confidence child rule for curl -F data=@ and /file/upload, described as exfiltration, so the alert text matches the direction of travel.

T1070

Cleanup as corroboration — removal of a staged archive is weak alone, but strong when it closes a chain that already showed staging and compression.

Tuning

Scope the noisy five — exclude cwd=/var/ossec from the netstat and df rules, allowlist cron and wazuh-modulesd for shadow reads, and restrict the systemctl rule to service-management verbs rather than --version.

Takeaway

A detection pack that only reports its hits is marketing. Three emulations produced nine working rules and a nine-item defect list — five uncovered behaviours, one alert describing the wrong direction, and five rules watching the monitoring stack instead of the adversary. None of that surfaces from reading rule definitions; it surfaces from running an adversary against them and scoring the result honestly. The chain a real intrusion follows — stage, archive, exfiltrate, erase — ran end to end through a monitored host and raised two alerts. Knowing that is worth more than the nine rules that worked.
